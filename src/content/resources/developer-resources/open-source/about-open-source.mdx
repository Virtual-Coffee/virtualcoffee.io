## What Is Open Source Software (OSS)?

Open Source Software (OSS) is software where the source code is freely available and accessible to the public. Its creators distribute it under a copyright license that permits reuse, modification, and redistribution and encourages open collaboration and peer production.

There are various licenses in open source. Some grant almost complete freedom, including incorporating code into commercial, closed-source projects. Others are more restrictive.

There is a culture in open source of building on each other's work. You can take an open source project and extend it or add it to another project as long as you comply with the terms of the license. Package managers like npm can also incorporate other open source code into your project.

The ecosystem of open source software is vast. It ranges from programs with a graphical interface, to libraries and modules that provide specific functionality to other programs, to frameworks and programming languages. It also includes the infrastructure behind decentralized social networks and blockchain protocols, and simple personal projects.

Although best known in software, open source concepts can extend to any field with intellectual property, such as hardware development, design systems, and designs for 3D printable goods.

## Building a Portfolio with Open Source

Doing open source work is a great way to improve your skills, build your portfolio, and attract the attention of others in the industry. And this is one of the reasons why you want to get involved in and contribute to open source.

Many employers value open source work because it requires skills you can use while working a tech job. Add your contributions to your resume, especially if you are looking for your first developer job. It helps you stand out from other candidates.

It is also worth remembering that contributing to open source is not always about writing code. Contributions such as improving documentation, marketing, community building, design, or illustration are equally valuable to projects.

## Funding and Governance Models in Open Source

### Funding Models

There are several models for funding the running and development of open source projects:

- Some projects start as personal projects of their creator. The creator pays the costs, and the creator and other volunteer contributors do the work. Sometimes these projects use GitHub Sponsors or other sites, allowing people to donate small amounts to cover costs or compensate the creator for their time.
- Larger projects with a team of maintainers or a non-profit may use the same funding model. Still, they often supplement this with more ways to donate. Maintainers and contributors to these projects are usually volunteers. Money raised typically covers costs such as website hosting or services the organization uses.
- A company or organization (the project's maintainer) fully funds some projects alongside its core business. These projects typically use a combination of paid team members and volunteer contributors to get work done. A paid in-house team entirely develops some projects, and these do not usually receive external contributions. But the maintainer releases these projects to the public under an open source license.
- Some projects form the core part of a commercial enterprise. The company typically makes money from providing support, paid licenses with additional capabilities, or paid services that incorporate the open source project. A paid in-house team usually does most of the work on these projects, but they may also accept external volunteer contributions.

### Governance Models

There are several models for the governance of open source projects:

- The most common model is the 'Benevolent Dictatorship', where a single project creator makes or leads the decision-making process. They may consult and take input from the community, but ultimately all authority rests with the benevolent dictator.
- Various non-profit organizational structures govern some projects. These structures commonly have at least an agreed-upon set of rules for decision-making. The organizations may run larger projects with a formal constitution, official positions, and a member or committee structure to administer the organization and make decisions.
- A single company backs some projects. The internal procedures of the company typically govern these projects. However, a few may have a legally separate organization allowing for community involvement or ownership of the project.
- A foundation with corporate or individual members interested in the project often governs projects such as programming languages or platform standards. They appoint a core team or committee that administers the organization and makes decisions.
- Distributed Autonomous Organizations (DAOs) are becoming popular in blockchain and cryptocurrency. While the details differ from project to project, these typically feature a governance token on a blockchain that people can transfer, buy, or sell. The holders can vote on proposals.
